AIR2-02-R0

Recall the separate meanings of g, h and f in A*. Then state the difference between an estimate that never overestimates and estimates that fit together across every edge.

What does an estimate add to the cost already paid?

A* ranks a frontier route by f=g+h. The quantity g is the known cost from the start. The estimate h predicts the remaining cost. Greedy best-first search uses h alone, so a promising-looking destination can distract it from an expensive journey. An admissible h never exceeds the true cheapest remaining cost. Consistency is a stronger local check: across a road u→v of cost c, require h(u)≤c+h(v). Set h(goal)=0.

Follow one complete example

If S→A costs2, A→G costs4 and S→G costs9, take h(A)=4 and h(G)=0. Greedy chooses G after S because its h is zero. A* compares A at f=6 with G at f=9 and first chooses A. Its next G entry costs6.

Why might a state need a second turn?

An admissible but inconsistent estimate can delay a cheap route to a state until after that state has already been expanded. A graph-search implementation that permanently closes the state may then miss the best solution. Reopening means expanding it again when a better g is found. This is an algorithm rule, not a change in the meaning of the heuristic.

Follow one complete example

Take S→A:4, S→B:1, B→A:1, A→G:4, with h(S)=0,h(A)=0,h(B)=5,h(G)=0. The estimates do not overestimate: B really is5 from G. A is expanded at g4 before B at f6. B later finds A at g2. Reopening A gives G at6; permanently closing A leaves the earlier G at8.

How do the pieces fit together?

Every problem asks you to make an object you can reason with: a frontier, a game tree, a truth assignment, a path in a network or a probability table. The method then tells you which change is allowed. A final check asks whether the result answers the original question: cheapest route, forced conclusion or normalized probability. Learning the name of a method helps only when you can make and check those steps yourself.
