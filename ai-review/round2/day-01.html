If the goal is already in the queue, why keep searching?

A route planner sees a direct road to the destination, but finding a route is different from finding the cheapest route. The frontier is the collection of routes found but not yet examined. Uniform-cost search, or UCS, removes the route with the smallest cost already paid. We call that cost g. In a finite graph with positive road costs, testing the goal when its cheapest entry is removed allows cheaper unfinished routes their turn first. Ignore old queue entries when a better route to the same state has replaced them.

Follow one complete example

Suppose S→G costs 12, S→A costs 3, and A→G costs 4. Removing S produces A:3 and G:12. Removing A improves G to 7. The next useful removal is G:7. Stopping when G first appeared would have returned 12 instead of 7.

When does counting roads answer the cost question?

Breadth-first search, or BFS, removes states in increasing number of roads from the start. A queue gives earlier discoveries their turn first. If every road costs the same positive amount, fewer roads also means lower total cost. With unequal costs that implication fails. For counting questions, say whether you count generated states or removed states, whether the root counts, and whether the first goal itself counts.

Follow one complete example

In a complete ternary tree with root depth zero and all goals at depth two, BFS removes 1 root and 3 depth-one nodes before touching a goal. Including the first goal gives 5 removals. Generating all nine depth-two nodes is a different event.

How do the pieces fit together?

Every problem asks you to make an object you can reason with: a frontier, a game tree, a truth assignment, a path in a network or a probability table. The method then tells you which change is allowed. A final check asks whether the result answers the original question: cheapest route, forced conclusion or normalized probability. Learning the name of a method helps only when you can make and check those steps yourself.
