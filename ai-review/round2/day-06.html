What changes when we learn that an event happened?

Consider a binary cause A and binary observation E. Before observing E, P(A) is the prior. For E=1, multiply each prior by the likelihood of that evidence under that value of A. These products are joint weights, not yet conditional probabilities. Their sum is the evidence probability. Divide each by that sum to obtain a posterior whose entries add to one.

Follow one complete example

Let P(A=1)=0.4, P(E=1|A=1)=0.7 and P(E=1|A=0)=0.2. The weights are0.4×0.7=0.28 and0.6×0.2=0.12. Their sum is0.40, so P(A=1|E=1)=0.28/0.40=0.7.

Why can the same answer require a much larger table?

A factor is a table indexed by a set of variables, called its scope. Multiplying factors uses the union of their scopes. Summing out a hidden variable removes that variable from the resulting scope. Different elimination orders can create different temporary tables even though exact arithmetic gives the same final query distribution. Count the product table before the sum as well as the table after it.

Follow one complete example

For factors f(U,V),g(V,W),h(W), all variables binary, eliminating V first requires a product over U,V,W with8 entries and leaves a factor over U,W with4. Factor h(W) does not involve V and can wait. Eliminating W first instead combines g and h on V,W with4 entries and leaves2 entries on V.

How do the pieces fit together?

Every problem asks you to make an object you can reason with: a frontier, a game tree, a truth assignment, a path in a network or a probability table. The method then tells you which change is allowed. A final check asks whether the result answers the original question: cheapest route, forced conclusion or normalized probability. Learning the name of a method helps only when you can make and check those steps yourself.
