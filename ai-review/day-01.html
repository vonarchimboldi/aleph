Finding a route: three ways to search

AI unit 1 · a first lesson in uninformed search

Imagine a map with several possible routes to your destination. A search algorithm gives you a rule for deciding which route to examine next. We will build three rules and see why they can return different answers.

Work through one explanation and its small question at a time. Use paper to record each step. Open a worked check after you attempt the question. If you need help, keep that visible in your work.

1. Describe the task before choosing a method

A search problem needs a starting state, a way to find possible next states, and a test for recognizing the goal. A state describes where you are in the problem. On a road map it can be the town you have reached.

A path is a sequence of connected steps. A path’s cost is the sum of the costs of its steps. The cost could represent money, distance or time.

Here the start is S and the goal is G. The arrows show allowed steps. There are two routes. The direct route S→G uses one step and costs5. The route S→A→G uses two steps and costs1+1=2.

Try one small question

Which route uses fewer steps? Which costs less? Explain why those answers differ.

Check your answer

The direct route uses fewer steps. The route through A costs less. Each road has its own cost, so counting roads does not necessarily measure total cost.

2. Keep a list of possibilities

After examining S, we know how to reach A and how to reach G. We need to remember both possibilities until we decide which to examine next. The list of discovered possibilities waiting to be examined is called the frontier.

To expand a state means to examine its possible next steps. Different search methods choose the next frontier item in different ways. That choice is the main difference between the methods below.

Try one small question

Immediately after expanding S in the map, which two states are on the frontier? What cost has been paid to reach each?

Check your answer

A is available at cost1 and G is available at cost5. Finding G is not yet a reason to declare the cheapest route found. The route through A still deserves examination.

3. Breadth-first search: try fewer steps first

Breadth-first search, or BFS, examines states reached in one step before states reached in two steps. It examines two-step possibilities before three-step possibilities. We call a state’s number of steps from the start its depth.

A queue helps keep that order. A queue removes the oldest item from the front. When you discover a new possibility, add it at the back.

For a small example, suppose S has neighbors A and B in that order. A leads to C. After expanding S, the queue is[A,B]. Remove A and add its newly discovered neighbor C. The queue becomes[B,C]. B is examined before C because B is only one step away.

On our original map, G is one step from S. BFS can therefore return the direct route, costing5. BFS finds a route with the fewest steps. When every step has the same positive cost, that is also a cheapest route.

Try one small question

In the queue example, why does adding C at the back keep B ahead of it? What would change if you always examined the newest item first?

Check your answer

B was discovered before C. Removing the oldest item preserves that order. Examining the newest item would follow the branch through A and C before returning to B.

4. Uniform-cost search: try the cheapest route so far

Uniform-cost search, or UCS, uses the total cost already paid to reach a frontier item. This cost is often written g. A priority queue lets the method remove the item with the smallest g.

Follow the original map step by step:

What we doWhat remains to examine
Expand SA at cost1; G at cost5
Expand A, the cheaper itemA new route to G costs1+1=2
Remove the cheapest route to GReturn S→A→G at cost2

A careful implementation replaces or ignores the older, more expensive entry for G. It does not return the first route merely because that route reaches the goal.

For the usual positive-cost setting, UCS returns a cheapest route when it tests for the goal as the cheapest frontier item is removed. On an infinite search space, a fixed positive lower bound on step costs is also important for the usual completeness guarantee.

AI01-Q1 · Work through the same map independently

List the UCS removal order and final goal cost. Explain why stopping when G is first discovered would be wrong.

Worked check

Remove S, then A, then G. The returned cost is2. G was first discovered at cost5, while A still offered a possible improvement.

5. Depth-first search: follow one branch

Depth-first search, or DFS, follows one branch until it cannot continue. It then returns to the most recent point with an untried option. A stack of unfinished choices, or recursive function calls, can record where to return.

Suppose S leads to A and B, and A leads to C. If DFS tries A first, it visits S,A,C before returning to examine B. This differs from the BFS order S,A,B,C.

A cycle can cause trouble. With edges A→B and B→A, a method that keeps following edges without remembering visited states may repeat forever. A visited set records states already encountered. On a finite graph, it lets DFS avoid repeatedly expanding them.

AI01-Q2 · Explain the cycle

A finite directed graph has a reachable cycle. Can DFS without a visited set run forever? What changes when it records visited states?

Worked check

It can run forever under an unfortunate choice of next edges. A visited set prevents repeated expansion of the same state. On a finite graph, graph DFS then finishes. It can find a path without finding a shortest path.

6. Count the operation the question asks for

Discovering a state, adding it to the frontier, removing it, and expanding it are different events. An exam question may count any one of them. State when the algorithm tests for the goal before tracing it.

Consider a full binary tree. The root is at depth0. There is1 node at depth0,2 at depth1,4 at depth2 and8 at depth3. BFS removes all shallower nodes before reaching depth3.

AI01-Q3 · Count removals

Every goal is at depth3 in that tree. BFS tests for a goal when removing a node. How many nodes does it remove before and including its first goal?

Worked check

It removes1+2+4=7 nodes above depth3. The next removal is the first goal. Total:8. Counting nodes generated would answer a different question.

How the methods connect

MethodNext item chosenWhat to watch

These are called uninformed methods because they do not use an estimate of the distance or cost still remaining to the goal. The next unit will teach how such an estimate can help choose the next possibility.
