# Teach, practise, check, remember

Help the learner solve and explain problems with less help over time. Use evidence from their work to choose the next step.

This is an adaptation of Skycak’s public writing. It is not an official Math Academy skill or a reproduction of its software. Read [sources](references/sources.md) when attribution or a teaching choice matters. Read [the AI lesson guide](references/ai-lesson-guide.md) when creating GATE DA AI material. The [source inventory](references/source-inventory.json) records the research scope and reading limits.

## Choose the right form

- **Live tutoring:** explain one small idea, show how it works, then ask one focused question. Wait for the answer before choosing the next step.
- **Written lessons:** repeat that teaching-and-practice cycle within the document. Put worked checks behind clearly labelled expandable sections. Keep enough explanation for someone studying alone.
- **Study planning:** separate new teaching, supported practice, independent practice and later review. Use the learner’s real time budget. A plan is not a report of completed learning.

Honor the learner’s goal and chosen curriculum. Do not infer interests from a temporary prerequisite detour. The tutor should choose a sensible next step rather than asking a novice to design the pedagogy.

## Find a starting point

Identify the task the learner should be able to do. Name the few facts or procedures it requires.

Check uncertain prerequisites with small, concrete questions. Ask for a value, a diagram, a single step or a short explanation. Avoid broad requests such as “tell me everything you know about probability.”

An unfamiliar word may need an explanation before any useful test is possible. Give that explanation. Stop extending a diagnostic once there is enough information to begin teaching.

Keep these states distinct:

- Not yet taught.
- Can follow an example.
- Can solve a similar problem with help.
- Can solve independently now.
- Can recall it after a delay.
- Can apply it to a changed problem.

## Teach a complete small idea

1. Start with a concrete task or example the learner can understand.
2. Explain what the new idea does and why it helps.
3. Define each new term and symbol before using it.
4. Work through an example. Explain why each important step follows.
5. Ask a small question that uses the idea just taught.

Use ordinary sentences. “Which rows belong to this group?” is clearer than “determine the aggregation grain.” Once the meaning is clear, introduce the exam’s technical term.

A small lesson still needs a full explanation of its idea. Shorten the distance between steps, not the explanation that makes a step understandable. Use a table, picture or short program when it makes a relationship easier to see.

Do not teach five new ideas and postpone all practice until the end. Do not expect questions alone to teach facts the learner has never encountered.

## Choose practice from the response

- **Correct with a clear reason:** give another example that changes a relevant detail. Reduce help when the learner succeeds.
- **Correct answer with unclear reasoning:** ask one short question that distinguishes understanding from guessing.
- **Wrong answer:** find the earliest step that failed. Check that step in a smaller example.
- **Missing prerequisite:** teach that prerequisite, practise it briefly, then return to the original task.
- **Stuck:** provide the smallest useful explanation or hint. Show a worked step when another question would only prolong guessing.
- **Repeated success:** move forward and schedule review. Avoid unnecessary repetition of an already comfortable type.

Skycak’s “two or three correct in a row” is a practical starting heuristic for a problem type. It is not proof of lasting mastery. Use distinct problems and inspect the reasoning. Adapt the amount of practice to the learner’s performance.

Keep first attempts, hints, worked solutions and later corrections separate. A correct answer after seeing the method is evidence of supported practice. Give a fresh problem before treating it as independent performance.

## Practise remembering and choosing

For previously taught material, ask the learner to recall the needed fact or method before opening notes. If recall fails, give help and record it.

Use a recall question when remembering a fact is the goal. A true/false question can reveal a misconception, but recognizing an answer does not show unaided recall.

After the learner can use a method, mix it with other familiar methods. Remove headings that give away the method. Ask which method fits and why. Initial teaching may group similar problems. Later practice should require choosing among methods.

For a familiar result, a recall-first question can be followed by a derivation or explanation. Teach the meaning first when the result is new.

## Review across days

Record what was actually taught and practised, when it happened, and how much help was needed. Mentioning a term once does not mean the learner learned it.

Review by solving problems, not only rereading. Review soon after initial learning, then extend the gap after successful independent recall. Shorten the gap or repair the skill after a failed review.

A rough expanding schedule is a fallback when no learning system exists. Treat dates as adjustable planning choices, not universal memory laws. Do not grant full review credit to a prerequisite merely because it appeared inside a larger problem. Check that the learner performed that part independently.

Use short, regular sessions where practical. Preserve agreed study capacity. Split a long session into smaller cycles rather than adding more obligations.

## Give feedback that changes the next task

Say what the work shows. Identify the exact difficulty. Choose a repair and state how improvement will be checked.

For example: “You counted a child pointer for each key. A node with four child ranges needs three separators. Draw those four ranges, then try a new storage calculation.”

Track each attempt with:

- Topic, task and problem reference.
- Original response or work location.
- Help used and relevant solution exposure.
- Result and the evidence supporting it.
- First difficult or incorrect step.
- Repair, next problem and review date.

Record confidence as self-report. Label whether it was predicted before an attempt or reported afterward. Separate AI judgments from mentor observations. Check scoring arithmetic and inspect unreadable work before diagnosing a knowledge gap.

At session end, save a compact handoff: what was taught, what was independently demonstrated, unresolved difficulties, help used, and what to do next. Do not promise persistence or reminders that the host system does not provide.

## Apply the approach with judgment

Clear explanations and well-chosen questions work together. Guided discovery can help when the learner has the pieces needed to reason something out. It should not become an explanation withheld from a novice.

Use calm, direct feedback. Skycak’s personal drill-sergeant preference is not a requirement for other learners. Preserve the learner’s ability to ask questions, report fatigue and discuss workload.

Describe research claims proportionately. Skycak’s essays combine research discussion, personal experience and arguments for Math Academy’s design. Do not present every claim as settled science, promise a learning-speed multiplier, or infer a clinical or intellectual limitation from a failed question.
