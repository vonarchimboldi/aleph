Answering a probability question by removing what we do not need

A network can describe many variables even when we only want one answer. Variable elimination combines and adds small tables so that we do not have to keep every variable until the end.

1. Separate the question from the information given

The query is the variable whose probability we want. Evidence is the information we are told. Hidden variables are variables we must account for but do not want in the final answer.

Use A→B, with P(A=1)=0.2, P(B=1|A=1)=0.8 and P(B=1|A=0)=0.1. We observe B=1 and ask for P(A=1|B=1). Here A is the query and B=1 is evidence.

The two possible causes A=1 and A=0 can both lead to the evidence. We must compare their probabilities of occurring together with B=1.

AI06-W1

Why is the second row’s first factor 0.8 rather than 0.2?

Worked check · open after attempting

That row describes A=0. Since A is binary, P(A=0)=1−P(A=1)=0.8.

2. Normalize the weights to obtain the posterior

The weights 0.16 and 0.08 describe probabilities before restricting all attention to the evidence. Their sum 0.24 is the probability of B=1.

To express probabilities within the B=1 cases, divide each weight by 0.24. This is normalization: scale the weights so they sum to one.

The resulting conditional distribution is called the posterior after seeing the evidence. Here P(A=1|B=1)=0.16/0.24=2/3 and P(A=0|B=1)=1/3. The numbers add to one, as probabilities for the two possible A values should.

Follow the reasoning

Of the 24 equally weighted evidence cases in a 100-case illustration, 16 have A=1 and 8 have A=0. The posterior compares 16 with 24, not with all 100.

3. A factor is a table indexed by some variables

A factor assigns a number to each combination of its listed variables. Its scope is the set of variables used to index that table. It need not itself sum to one.

For example, a factor f(A,B) over two binary variables has four entries: f(0,0),f(0,1),f(1,0),f(1,1). A conditional probability table can be used as a factor.

To multiply factors, match rows that agree on shared variables and multiply their values. The product is indexed by the union of their variables. To enter evidence, keep only entries matching the observed value.

Follow the reasoning

Suppose f(A=1,B=0)=0.2 and g(B=0,C=1)=0.7. Their contribution at A=1,B=0,C=1 is 0.2×0.7=0.14. If B=1 is evidence, this B=0 combination is excluded.

4. Sum a hidden variable out of the table

Suppose we want a table over A and C, but our product still includes hidden B. For each fixed A,C pair, add the entry for B=0 to the entry for B=1. B then disappears from the table’s scope.

This operation is called summing out or eliminating B. It accounts for both possible B values. Taking the larger entry instead would answer a different question.

For the chain A→B→C with observed C=c, the unnormalized query table is f(A)=P(A) times the sum over b of P(b|A)P(c|b). Normalize this final table over A.

Follow the reasoning

Add P(C=1|B=1)=0.9 and P(C=1|B=0)=0.2 to our model. Given A=1, the two B contributions are 0.8×0.9 and 0.2×0.2. They sum to 0.76. Multiplying by P(A=1)=0.2 gives weight 0.152.

For A=0, the contributions are 0.1×0.9 and 0.9×0.2, summing to 0.27. Its weight is 0.8×0.27=0.216. Their sum is 0.368, so P(A=1|C=1)=0.152/0.368=19/46.

For A→B→C with evidence C=c, write the unnormalized factor for query A after eliminating hidden B.

Worked check · open after attempting

f(A)=P(A) Σ_b P(b|A)P(c|b). Normalize f(A) over A to obtain the posterior.

5. Multiply only the factors needed for an elimination

Suppose the current factors are f(X,Y),g(Y,Z) and h(Z). To eliminate Y, first gather the factors containing Y. These are f and g. The factor h does not need to participate yet.

The product f(X,Y)g(Y,Z) has scope {X,Y,Z}. With binary variables, that product has eight possible indexed entries. Summing over Y produces a factor on {X,Z}, with four entries.

You can then multiply the result by h(Z). Moving h outside the sum is valid because h does not depend on Y. Implementations can combine multiplication and summation without storing every product entry, but the scopes explain the operation.

Follow the reasoning

For one fixed pair X=x,Z=z, calculate k(x,z)=f(x,0)g(0,z)+f(x,1)g(1,z). Repeat for the four X,Z pairs.

6. Choose an elimination order that keeps tables small

Different elimination orders give the same exact probability when the operations are performed correctly. They can create very different intermediate table sizes.

Before eliminating a variable, write the scope of the factors that contain it. Their union gives the variables in the product. Remove the eliminated variable to find the resulting scope.

For example, eliminating a highly connected variable early can create a large factor connecting its neighbors. Count those table entries before deciding the order. The goal is to avoid unnecessarily large tables, not to change the answer.

Follow the reasoning

A table on six binary variables has 64 entries. A table on three has 8. Reducing the largest intermediate scope can make a large difference even when the final query has only two answers.

How the ideas connect

  • Identify query, evidence and hidden variables.
  • Restrict factors to evidence before unnecessary work.
  • Multiply matching entries, then add over hidden-variable values.
  • Normalize the final weights and check they sum to one.
