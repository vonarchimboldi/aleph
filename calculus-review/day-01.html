Opening check · before reading

What is wrong with saying that 0/0 is the answer to a limit?

What are we trying to find?

Imagine watching the values of a function while its input approaches a point. A limit asks what output those nearby values approach. It does not require the function to be defined at the point itself. For example, a missing point in a graph can still have a definite height that the surrounding graph approaches.

Start by substitution. If the expression is continuous at the target and is defined there, substitution settles the problem. If it produces 0/0, you have learned that both numerator and denominator vanish. You have not learned the ratio of their rates of vanishing. We need to reveal that ratio by rewriting the expression.

Choose the rewrite for a reason

A polynomial quotient suggests factoring. A difference of square roots suggests its conjugate, because multiplying a sum and a difference removes the roots. Trigonometric expressions near zero suggest the standard limit lim⁡t→0sin⁡t/t=1\lim_{t\to0}\sin t/t=1, with angles measured in radians. At infinity, divide by the largest relevant power to compare growth.

For two-sided limits, inspect both sides. The function ∣x∣/x|x|/x equals 1 for positive x and −1 for negative x. No single number describes both approaches to zero. Writing “the denominator tends to zero” does not tell you whether a quotient diverges, has a finite limit, or has different one-sided behaviors.

One useful bound is −x2≤x2sin⁡(1/x)≤x2-x^2\le x^2\sin(1/x)\le x^2 for nonzero x. Both outer quantities tend to zero, so the middle one does too. This squeeze argument does not require the oscillating sine factor to have a limit.

L’Hôpital’s rule is a conditional tool, not permission to differentiate every numerator and denominator. For the version used here, the quotient must have a 0/0 or infinity/infinity form, the functions must be differentiable in a deleted neighborhood on the side considered, the denominator derivative must be nonzero there, and the derivative quotient must have an applicable limit. Try an algebraic rewrite first in this refresher.

The full map of single-variable limit questions

There are two different choices to make. First: does the input approach a finite point, +∞, or −∞? Second: does the output approach a finite number, grow without bound, or fail to settle? A one-sided question also specifies which approach is allowed. “Infinite limit” and “limit at infinity” are not synonyms: the first describes the output; the second describes the input.

Here is the coverage checklist, using Stewart’s single-variable limits and indeterminate-forms sections as a scope reference: graph/table reading; numerical evidence versus proof; limit laws and direct substitution; factoring and cancellation; conjugates; one-sided, absolute-value and piecewise limits; squeeze and oscillation; continuity and removable discontinuities; finite-input infinite limits and vertical asymptotes; limits at positive and negative infinity, horizontal asymptotes and growth; all seven standard indeterminate forms; derivative-definition limits; and the precise epsilon–delta definition. This is a reference library across the common types, not a claim that one example exhausts every possible problem.

Why an indeterminate form asks for another step

The forms 0/0 and ∞/∞ describe competing quantities. Forms 0·∞ and ∞−∞ must be rewritten into a quotient or simplified by a common denominator or conjugate. For 0⁰, 1^∞, and ∞⁰, take a logarithm of a positive expression and study the resulting product. These labels do not assign values: the same form can lead to different limits.

For L’Hôpital’s rule, keep the differentiability and denominator-derivative conditions stated above. Its input must first be an eligible quotient. A form like 1^∞ does not allow you to differentiate the base and exponent separately.

What epsilon–delta adds to the picture

To say f(x) approaches L as x approaches a means this: for every tolerance ε>0 on the output, there is a tolerance δ>0 on the input such that 0<|x−a|<δ forces |f(x)−L|<ε. The output tolerance is chosen first; our δ may depend on it. The input is then allowed to be any point satisfying the restriction. This order of choices matters.

A table of values can suggest a limit, but finitely many samples cannot rule out a spike or oscillation between samples. A proof gives control over every sufficiently close input. The worked examples below show how to get that control or how to expose its failure.

Two worked examples · follow the decisions

Example 1 · A hole in a rational function

Find lim⁡x→2(x2−4)/(x−2)\lim_{x\to2}(x^2-4)/(x-2).

Substitution gives 0/0. Factor the numerator: x2−4=(x−2)(x+2)x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2). For x near 2 but not equal to 2, cancellation is legitimate, so the quotient equals x+2. Its nearby values therefore approach 4. The original quotient remains undefined at 2; the limit is nevertheless 4.

Example 2 · Why a conjugate helps

Find lim⁡x→0(1+x−1)/x\lim_{x\to0}(\sqrt{1+x}-1)/x.

The square root is defined near zero, and substitution gives 0/0. Multiply numerator and denominator by 1+x+1\sqrt{1+x}+1:

1+x−1x=xx(1+x+1)=11+x+1(x≠0).\frac{\sqrt{1+x}-1}{x}=\frac{x}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}=\frac1{\sqrt{1+x}+1}\quad(x\ne0).

The new denominator tends to 2, so the limit is 1/2. We cancelled only away from zero, which is exactly where a limit examines the function.

Example 3 · Read the approach, not just the plotted point

A graph follows y=x+1 for x≠1, but its filled point at x=1 is (1,7). Find its limit and its value at 1.

The nearby graph approaches height 2 from both sides, so the limit is 2. The filled point gives f(1)=7. The function is discontinuous at 1. Changing only the point value can repair continuity without changing the surrounding limit.

Example 4 · A piecewise continuity parameter

Let f(x)=ax+1 for x<1, and f(x)=x²+2 for x≥1. Find a making f continuous at 1.

The left limit is a+1; the right limit and the value are 3. Matching these gives a=2. We needed both one-sided limits and the actual point value to agree.

Example 5 · A finite input with an infinite output

Find the one-sided limits of 1/(x−2) at 2 and compare them with those of 1/(x−2)².

For 1/(x−2), the left limit is −∞ and the right is +∞, so there is no common two-sided limit even in the extended sense. For 1/(x−2)², both sides approach +∞. In both cases x=2 is a vertical asymptote; infinity describes unbounded behavior, not an ordinary real function value.

Example 6 · Negative infinity and the absolute value hidden in a square root

Find the limits of √(x²+1)/x as x→+∞ and as x→−∞.

Write √(x²+1)=|x|√(1+1/x²). The quotient is (|x|/x)√(1+1/x²). The second factor tends to 1, while the first is +1 on the positive side and −1 on the negative side. The answers are 1 and −1. Replacing √(x²) with x on negative inputs would lose the sign.

Example 7 · Infinity minus infinity: reveal the cancellation

Find lim as x→+∞ of √(x²+x)−x.

Multiply by the conjugate: the difference equals x/[√(x²+x)+x]. For positive x, divide top and bottom by x to obtain 1/[√(1+1/x)+1], which tends to 1/2. The form ∞−∞ did not mean zero.

Example 8 · Zero times infinity: make an eligible quotient

Find lim as x→0+ of x ln x.

Write ln x/(1/x), an ∞/∞ quotient in magnitude on x>0. L’Hôpital gives (1/x)/(−1/x²)=−x→0. Equivalently set t=1/x and compare ln t with t. The limit is 0 from below.

Example 9 · Exponential versus polynomial growth

Find lim as x→+∞ of x²/eˣ.

This is ∞/∞. Two justified applications of L’Hôpital give first 2x/eˣ and then 2/eˣ, whose limit is 0. This expresses the faster growth of the exponential, not cancellation of infinities.

Example 10 · The form 1 to infinity

Find lim as x→0 of (1+x)^(1/x), with x>−1 and x≠0.

Let y=(1+x)^(1/x)>0. Then ln y=ln(1+x)/x. This quotient is 0/0, and L’Hôpital gives 1/(1+x)→1. Continuity of exp then gives y→e. Taking logarithms turned the power into a quotient.

Example 11 · The form zero to zero

Find lim as x→0+ of xˣ.

For y=xˣ, ln y=x ln x→0 by the earlier example. Thus y→exp(0)=1. This limit does not define a universal arithmetic value for 0⁰.

Example 12 · The form infinity to zero

Find lim as x→+∞ of x^(1/x).

Its logarithm is (ln x)/x, an ∞/∞ quotient whose derivative ratio is 1/x→0. Exponentiating gives limit 1.

Example 13 · A quotient is still indeterminate after one rewrite

Find lim as x→0 of (1−cos x)/x².

Use 1−cos x=2sin²(x/2). The quotient becomes one half times [sin(x/2)/(x/2)]². The bracket tends to 1, giving 1/2. This uses the standard trigonometric limit with the correct scale.

Example 14 · An oscillation that cannot be squeezed to one value

Does sin(1/x) have a limit at zero?

Take xₙ=1/(π/2+2πn) and yₙ=1/(3π/2+2πn). Both sequences of inputs tend to zero, but their outputs are 1 and −1. The two incompatible limiting outputs rule out a limit. In contrast, multiplication by x² shrinks the oscillation to zero.

Example 15 · A derivative hidden in a limit

For differentiable f, evaluate lim as h→0 of [f(a+2h)−f(a)]/h.

Set t=2h. The quotient is 2[f(a+t)−f(a)]/t, so its limit is 2f′(a). The denominator must match the actual increment before invoking the derivative definition.

Example 16 · A complete epsilon–delta proof

Prove directly that 3x+2 approaches 8 as x approaches 2.

Let ε>0 be arbitrary and choose δ=ε/3. If 0<|x−2|<δ, then |(3x+2)−8|=3|x−2|<3δ=ε. The choice works for every allowed nearby input, which is what a numerical table cannot establish.

Problems 1–3 · Concept and mechanics

C01-P1

Compute (a) lim at x=2 of (x²−4)/(x−2), (b) lim at x=0 of (√(9+x)−3)/x, and (c) lim at x=0 of sin(5x)/(2x). State the recognition cue for each.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

(a) Factor and cancel away from 2: answer 4. (b) Conjugate gives 1/[√(9+x)+3]: answer 1/6. (c) Match the argument 5x in the standard sine ratio: answer 5/2.

C01-P2

Find (a) the two one-sided limits of |x|/x at zero, (b) the two one-sided limits of 1/x², and (c) the limit of √(x²+4)/x as x→−∞.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

(a) Left −1, right 1, so no two-sided limit. (b) Both +∞; no finite real limit. (c) √(x²+4)/x=(|x|/x)√(1+4/x²)→−1.

C01-P3

Compute (a) lim at x→+∞ of √(x²+4x)−x, (b) lim at x→0+ of x ln x, and (c) lim at x→0 of (1+2x)^(1/x). Identify the original forms.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

(a) ∞−∞: conjugate and divide by positive x to get 4/[√(1+4/x)+1]→2. (b) 0·(−∞): rewrite as ln x/(1/x), giving limit 0. (c) 1^∞: its logarithm ln(1+2x)/x tends to 2, so answer e².

Problems 4–5 · Application and explanation

C01-P4

Explain why x sin(1/x) has limit zero but sin(1/x) does not. Then give a δ that proves 5x−1 approaches 9 as x approaches 2.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The first has absolute value at most |x|, so squeeze gives zero. The second has approaches giving outputs 1 and −1. For the linear proof, choose δ=ε/5 because |(5x−1)−9|=5|x−2|.

C01-P5

Compute (a) lim at x=0 of (1−cos 2x)/x² and (b) lim at h=0 of [f(a+3h)−f(a)]/h for differentiable f. Can a finite table prove either limit?

Worked solution — open after an attempt

(a) Rewrite using 2sin²x to obtain 2. (b) Set t=3h and obtain 3f′(a). A table is evidence, not a proof for all sufficiently close inputs.

Exit check · close examples and solutions

Without notes, find lim⁡x→0(4+x−2)/x\lim_{x\to0}(\sqrt{4+x}-2)/x. Explain which equality requires x≠0.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

Multiply by the conjugate to get 1/(4+x+2)1/(\sqrt{4+x}+2) for x≠0. The limit is 1/4. Cancelling x requires x≠0.

The big picture · reconstruct the map

Text version of the map
  • What approaches?: Finite point; +∞; −∞; Check left and right.
  • What happens to output?: Finite value; unbounded; Oscillation or incompatible sides.
  • Reveal structure: Factor; conjugate; squeeze; Match trig/derivative increments.
  • Indeterminate forms: Rewrite products/differences; Log powers; eligible L’Hôpital.
  • Justify the conclusion: Laws and hypotheses; ε–δ controls every nearby input.
