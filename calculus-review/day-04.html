Opening check · before reading

Does a sign change prove a zero when the function has a jump?

A graph cannot cross a height without meeting it—if it stays connected

Imagine a continuous temperature record that starts below 20 and ends above 20. It must equal 20 somewhere in between. The intermediate value theorem, or IVT, is this statement for a continuous real function on a closed interval.

Precisely: if f is continuous on [a,b], every value k between f(a) and f(b) occurs as f(c) for some c in [a,b]. If k lies strictly between the endpoint values, a solution lies in (a,b). To prove a zero, it is convenient to show f(a)f(b)<0.

This is an existence theorem. It does not tell you the exact location or the number of solutions. A function may cross zero three times while changing sign between its endpoints. To show uniqueness, use additional information, such as strict monotonicity.

The theorem’s continuity requirement is essential. On [−1,1], the function equal to −1 for x<0 and 1 for x≥0 changes sign but never equals zero. The graph jumps over the target. Also, no sign change does not mean there is no root: x² has a zero at 0 even though it is positive at both −1 and 1.

For a parameter question, construct the function whose zero expresses the desired equation. To solve f(x)=g(x), study h(x)=f(x)−g(x). To prove a fixed point f(c)=c, study h(x)=f(x)−x. These are translations from the question into the form the theorem can use.

The following twelve examples are an original IVT application ladder. For each, identify the continuous function, the interval, the target, and the endpoint information before reading the argument. Examples 8–10 are extensions. The aim is to learn how to construct the function to which IVT applies.

Two worked examples · follow the decisions

Example 1 · 1 · A polynomial root in a specified interval

Prove x³+x−1 has a root in (0,1).

Its polynomial left side is continuous on [0,1]; its endpoint values are −1 and 1. IVT supplies an interior zero. We have proved existence, not uniqueness.

Example 2 · 2 · A transcendental crossing

Prove cos x=x has a solution in (0,1).

Set h(x)=cos x−x. It is continuous, h(0)=1>0, and h(1)=cos1−1<0. IVT gives a zero of h, which is the original equality. The useful function was the difference.

Example 3 · 3 · Every target value of an odd polynomial

For any real k, show x³+x=k has a real solution.

Fix k and put h(x)=x³+x−k. Choose R>|k|+1. Then h(R)>0 and h(−R)<0. Continuity gives a zero between −R and R. The interval may depend on the chosen target k.

Example 4 · 4 · A fixed point of a self-map

If continuous f maps [0,1] into [0,1], prove f(c)=c somewhere.

Use h(x)=f(x)−x. Its endpoint values satisfy h(0)≥0 and h(1)≤0. If an endpoint is zero we are done; otherwise strict opposite signs give an interior zero. A non-strict sign condition allows an endpoint solution.

Example 5 · 5 · Two roots require two brackets

Prove x²+x−1 has two distinct real zeros using IVT.

The values at −2,0,1 are 1,−1,1. Continuity yields one root in (−2,0) and another in (0,1). The disjoint intervals ensure distinctness. No conclusion about “exactly two” follows from these two IVT applications alone; the quadratic degree can supply an upper bound.

Example 6 · 6 · A parameter family without solving it

For every real a, prove x³+ax−1 has a real root.

Fix a and choose R>|a|+2. Then R³−|a|R−1>0. Hence h(R)>0 and h(−R)<0 for h(x)=x³+ax−1. The polynomial is continuous, so some zero lies between them. Explicit algebraic root formulas are unnecessary.

Example 7 · 7 · Bisection retains a certified root

Bracket a root of x³+x−1 on [0,1], then take one bisection.

The endpoint signs are opposite. At 1/2 the value is 1/8+1/2−1=−3/8. The retained interval [1/2,1] still has opposite signs and continuity, so IVT certifies a root there. The midpoint decision is based on sign, not on which endpoint looks visually nearer.

Example 8 · 8 · Equal endpoint values and a shifted argument

If continuous f on [0,2] satisfies f(0)=f(2), prove some c∈[0,1] has f(c)=f(c+1).

Let h(x)=f(x)−f(x+1) on [0,1]. Then h(0)=f(0)−f(1) and h(1)=f(1)−f(2)=−h(0). If these are zero, an endpoint works; otherwise use IVT. The auxiliary function exposes opposite signs that were not visible in f alone.

Example 9 · 9 · Equal temperatures at opposite points

A temperature T is continuous around a circle. Show there are opposite points with equal temperature.

Parameterize the circle with a continuous 2π-periodic T(θ). Let h(θ)=T(θ)−T(θ+π). Then h(π)=−h(0). IVT on [0,π] gives a zero, including an endpoint if applicable. This is a one-dimensional argument, not a claim about discontinuous temperature assignments.

Example 10 · 10 · Averages of a continuous function are attained

If f is continuous on [a,b], a<b, show some c has f(c)=(1/(b−a))∫ₐᵇf(x)dx.

Continuity on a compact interval gives a minimum m and maximum M. Integrating m≤f≤M and dividing by b−a puts the average between m and M. IVT then makes f attain that average somewhere. This uses the extreme value theorem and integral bounds as well as IVT; it is not the derivative MVT.

Example 11 · 11 · A root missed by endpoint sign change

Why does f(x)=x² on [−1,1] show that opposite endpoint signs are not necessary for a root?

Both endpoint values are 1, yet f(0)=0. Opposite signs plus continuity are sufficient. The converse “no sign change, no zero” is false. This example trains the direction of the implication.

Example 12 · 12 · Failure when the graph can jump

Why do the endpoint values of 1/x on [−1,1] not prove a zero?

The function is undefined at zero and is not continuous on the interval. It takes values −1 and 1 at the endpoints but never equals zero. The missing hypothesis is precisely what would have prevented jumping across the target.

Problems 1–3 · Concept and mechanics

C04-P1

Prove x⁵+x−3 has a zero in (1,2).

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The polynomial is continuous. At 1 it is −1; at 2 it is 31. Zero lies strictly between them, so IVT gives a root in (1,2).

C04-P2

Prove e^(−x)=x has a solution in (0,1).

Worked solution — open after an attempt

Let h(x)=e^(−x)−x. It is continuous, h(0)=1>0 and h(1)=e⁻¹−1<0. IVT gives a zero of h, hence the required equality.

C04-P3

Give a continuous function with a zero on [1,3] but positive values at both endpoints.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

f(x)=(x−2)² works: both endpoint values are 1 and f(2)=0. Opposite endpoint signs are sufficient, not necessary.

Problems 4–5 · Application and explanation

C04-P4

On [−1,1], let g(x)=−2 for x<0 and g(x)=2 for x≥0. Do its endpoint signs guarantee a zero? Explain.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

No. The function is discontinuous at zero, so IVT does not apply. Its only values are −2 and 2, neither of which is zero.

C04-P5

Prove x+ln x=0 has exactly one positive root.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

On [1/e,1], h(x)=x+ln x is continuous, with h(1/e)=1/e−1<0 and h(1)=1>0, giving existence. Its derivative 1+1/x>0 on (0,∞), so it is strictly increasing and the positive root is unique.

Exit check · close examples and solutions

A student says “f(0)<0<f(1), so there is exactly one root.” Identify both missing justifications.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

Continuity on [0,1] is needed for the IVT existence argument. Uniqueness needs a separate condition or proof, such as strict monotonicity.

If something broke

If existence was read as uniqueness, compare x and x(x²−1/4) on [−1,1]: both change endpoint sign, but have different numbers of zeros. If continuity was omitted, retry the 1/x counterexample.

The big picture · reconstruct the map

Text version of the map
  • Hypothesis: Continuity on one interval; Target between endpoint values.
  • Translate the question: Root: h=f−k; crossing: h=f−g; Fixed point: h=f−x.
  • Conclusion: At least one attained target; Not a formula or uniqueness claim.
  • Applications: Brackets; symmetry; fixed points; Averages; parameter families.
  • Boundary of the theorem: Jumps can skip values; No sign change can still have roots.
