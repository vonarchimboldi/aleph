Opening check · before reading

Does a bounded real sequence have to converge? Give an example or a proof.

Being trapped is not the same as settling

A sequence is a list a₁,a₂,… indexed by positive integers. Convergence to L means that every requested tolerance ε>0 eventually traps all later terms within distance ε of L. The word “all” matters. For (−1)ⁿ, infinitely many terms equal 1 and infinitely many equal −1. The sequence is bounded but does not converge.

A subsequence chooses terms at increasing indices n₁<n₂<…. Choosing the even indices of (−1)ⁿ gives the constant subsequence 1; odd indices give −1. A convergent subsequence can exist even when the original sequence fails to settle.

Monotone convergence adds a direction of travel

A sequence is nondecreasing if aₙ₊₁≥aₙ for every n. If it is also bounded above, it converges. Its limit is the supremum, or least upper bound, of its terms. A nonincreasing sequence bounded below also converges, to its infimum.

Here is the proof for the increasing case. Let L be the supremum of the set of terms. No term exceeds L. Given any ε>0, L−ε cannot be an upper bound, because it is smaller than the least upper bound. Therefore some term aₙ₀ exceeds L−ε. Monotonicity keeps every later term at least that large, while L remains an upper bound. Thus all later terms satisfy L−ε<aₙ≤L. That is convergence to L.

Notice the roles: boundedness supplies a finite supremum; monotonicity makes entry into a small neighborhood permanent. Without boundedness, aₙ=n increases forever without a finite limit. Without monotonicity, bounded oscillation is possible.

Bolzano–Weierstrass promises less, under fewer assumptions

Every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence. It does not promise convergence of the whole sequence. To picture why, enclose all terms in a closed interval and bisect it. At least one half contains infinitely many indexed terms. Keep such a half and repeat. Choose a later indexed term in each retained interval. The nested intervals have lengths tending to zero and share a point L; the chosen subsequence approaches L. An unbounded sequence may still have a convergent subsequence, but boundedness guarantees one.

Here “monotone convergence” concerns real sequences. It is not the measure-theoretic monotone convergence theorem for integrals.

Two worked examples · follow the decisions

Example 1 · Establish convergence before solving for a limit

Let a₁=1 and an+1=2+ana_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}. Prove convergence and find its limit.

First prove by induction that 1≤aₙ≤2. If this holds for aₙ, then √3≤aₙ₊₁≤2, so the bounds persist. Next compare aₙ₊₁ and aₙ. Both are nonnegative; squaring shows aₙ₊₁≥aₙ exactly when 2+aₙ≥aₙ², or (2−aₙ)(aₙ+1)≥0. Our bounds make this true. The sequence is nondecreasing and bounded, so it converges to a real L in [1,2]. Continuity of the square root permits L=√(2+L), hence (L−2)(L+1)=0. Only L=2 fits the bounds. Solving the equation was the final step, not the proof of convergence.

Example 2 · One sequence, two subsequential limits

Analyze aₙ=(−1)ⁿ+1/n.

The sequence is bounded. On even indices a₂ₖ=1+1/(2k) tends to 1. On odd indices a₂ₖ₋₁=−1+1/(2k−1) tends to −1. If the whole sequence converged, every subsequence would have that same limit. The two different limits therefore prove divergence of the full sequence.

Problems 1–3 · Concept and mechanics

C09-P1

Prove aₙ=1−1/n converges using monotonicity and boundedness, and find its limit.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

aₙ₊₁−aₙ=1/[n(n+1)]>0, and aₙ<1. Monotone convergence applies; the direct expression gives limit 1.

C09-P2

Give a nondecreasing sequence with no finite limit. Which hypothesis is missing?

Worked solution — open after an attempt

aₙ=n is nondecreasing but not bounded above. Monotone convergence requires the appropriate finite bound.

C09-P3

Give a bounded divergent sequence and two convergent subsequences.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

Use (−1)ⁿ. Even terms give limit 1; odd terms give limit −1. Boundedness guarantees a subsequence, not full convergence.

Problems 4–5 · Application and explanation

C09-P4

For a₁=0 and aₙ₊₁=(aₙ+2)/2, prove convergence and find the limit.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

Inductively 0≤aₙ<2. The difference aₙ₊₁−aₙ=(2−aₙ)/2>0. Hence convergence follows. Taking limits gives L=(L+2)/2, so L=2.

C09-P5

If a real sequence converges to L, explain why every subsequence converges to L.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

For any ε>0 choose N so all n≥N have |aₙ−L|<ε. Increasing positive indices nₖ satisfy nₖ≥k, so for k≥N their selected terms meet the same bound.

Exit check · close examples and solutions

Explain why the fixed-point equation L=√(2+L), by itself, does not prove convergence of the recurrence in the worked example.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The equation states a condition on a limit if one exists. Boundedness and monotonicity first prove existence. Only afterward may continuity justify passing to the limit and selecting the admissible root.

If something broke

If boundedness was confused with convergence, revisit even and odd subsequences. If a recurrence was solved without proof of convergence, separate bounds, monotonicity, existence, and limit identification. Fresh repair: aₙ=n/(n+1) is increasing, bounded above by 1, and converges to 1.
