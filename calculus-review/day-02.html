Opening check · before reading

Differentiate ln(1+x²). Which factor appears because of the chain rule?

Integration asks for accumulated change

An antiderivative F of f satisfies F′=f. An indefinite integral asks for all such functions: ∫f(x) dx=F(x)+C\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C. The constant matters because differentiation erases constants. A definite integral adds signed contributions over an interval. When f is continuous, the fundamental theorem gives ∫abf(x) dx=F(b)−F(a)\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a).

Do not choose a technique by the length of the expression. Look for structure. If you see a function together with its derivative, substitution may reverse the chain rule. If a product contains a factor that simplifies when differentiated, integration by parts may help. If the integrand is rational, compare degrees and factor the denominator before trying partial fractions.

The substitution u=g(x) includes du=g′(x) dxdu=g'(x)\,dx. A missing constant multiplier must be accounted for. In a definite integral you can change the bounds into u-values and remain in u throughout; do not mix old x-bounds with a new variable.

Integration by parts reverses the product rule: since (uv)′=u′v+uv′(uv)'=u'v+uv', integrating and rearranging gives ∫u dv=uv−∫v du\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du. This is useful only when the new integral is easier. “Choose u” is a decision about simplification, not a ritual.

Improper integrals require a limit. An antiderivative cannot be evaluated across a singularity as though the integrand were continuous. For example, ∫011/x dx=lim⁡ε↓0(−ln⁡ε)=+∞\int_0^1 1/x\,dx=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}(-\ln\varepsilon)=+\infty. The integral diverges.

A small toolbox to recognize before choosing a method

For p≠−1, the antiderivative of xᵖ is xᵖ⁺¹/(p+1). For p=−1, use ln|x| on an interval not crossing zero. The antiderivatives of eˣ, cos x, and sin x are eˣ, sin x, and −cos x. Also, 1/(1+x²) integrates to arctan x. Check each by differentiating.

A trigonometric power may need an identity first. For example, sin²x=(1−cos2x)/2. Therefore its integral from 0 to π is [x/2−sin2x/4] from 0 to π, which equals π/2. This is a rewrite followed by elementary integration, rather than a new integration technique.

Three kinds of integral, and why the distinctions matter

An indefinite integral is a family of antiderivatives, with +C on each connected interval. A proper definite integral is a number obtained by accumulation over a finite interval where the integrand is bounded and integrable. An improper integral is defined by limits because an endpoint is infinite or the integrand is unbounded. The same-looking antiderivative computation has different obligations in these three settings.

For improper integrals there are two standard sources: an infinite interval, such as ∫₁^∞x⁻²dx, and an unbounded integrand, such as ∫₀¹(1/√x)dx. A singularity inside an interval must be split into two one-sided improper integrals, and both must converge separately. Symmetric cancellation is a Cauchy principal value, not ordinary improper convergence.

A strategy tree for integration

First simplify algebra and check the domain. Then ask whether substitution reverses a chain rule, whether parts simplifies a product, whether trigonometric identities reveal a substitution, whether a quadratic radical suggests trigonometric substitution, or whether a rational expression calls for polynomial division followed by partial fractions. In partial fractions, repeated linear factors need every power, and an irreducible quadratic needs a linear numerator. Some integrals have no elementary antiderivative; a numerical approximation or a definite-integral identity may still answer the question.

Useful definite-integral symmetries: an odd integrand on [−a,a] integrates to zero, while an even integrand gives twice the integral from 0 to a. Under x↦a+b−x the endpoints swap, which often pairs a complicated integrand with a simpler complement. These are properties of signed integration; use absolute differences for geometric area.

The Leibniz rule: two related ideas

If only the endpoints depend on a parameter t, the fundamental theorem and chain rule give G′(t)=f(b(t))b′(t)−f(a(t))a′(t). If the integrand also depends on t, an extra contribution comes from its change throughout the interval:

ddt∫a(t)b(t)f(x,t) dx=f(b(t),t)b′(t)−f(a(t),t)a′(t)+∫a(t)b(t)∂f∂t(x,t) dx.\frac{d}{dt}\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}f(x,t)\,dx=f(b(t),t)b'(t)-f(a(t),t)a'(t)+\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)\,dx.

On a finite domain, continuity of f and its parameter partial derivative on a rectangle containing the relevant ranges, together with continuously differentiable endpoints, is a convenient sufficient condition. Improper domains require stronger control, such as an integrable bound on the derivative locally in t. Do not exchange differentiation and integration simply because the notation permits it.

The “Leibniz trick,” or differentiation under the integral sign, chooses a parameter so that differentiating makes a difficult integral easier. Recover the original integral by integrating the resulting parameter derivative and using a known parameter value. The worked example below carries out all three steps.

Two worked examples · follow the decisions

Example 1 · Reverse a chain rule

Compute ∫012x/(1+x2) dx\int_0^1 2x/(1+x^2)\,dx.

The denominator has derivative 2x, exactly the numerator. Put u=1+x², so du=2x dx. At x=0, u=1; at x=1, u=2. The integral becomes ∫12du/u=ln⁡2\int_1^2du/u=\ln2. Differentiating ln(1+x²) confirms the substitution.

Example 2 · Simplify one factor

Compute ∫xex dx\int xe^x\,dx.

Differentiate x to simplify it, and integrate eˣ without making it more complicated. Set u=x and dv=eˣdx, giving du=dx and v=eˣ. Then ∫xexdx=xex−∫exdx=(x−1)ex+C\int xe^x dx=xe^x-\int e^x dx=(x-1)e^x+C. Differentiating gives eˣ+(x−1)eˣ=xeˣ.

Example 3 · Trigonometric powers: save a derivative factor

Compute ∫sin³x cos x dx.

Write the integrand as (sin x)³ times cos x. Put u=sin x, du=cos x dx. The integral is u⁴/4+C=sin⁴x/4+C. For an even power without an available derivative factor, a half-angle identity may be more useful.

Example 4 · A quadratic radical suggests trigonometric substitution

Compute ∫dx/√(4−x²) on (−2,2).

Put x=2sinθ with θ in (−π/2,π/2), so dx=2cosθ dθ and √(4−x²)=2cosθ. The quotient becomes dθ. Thus the answer is arcsin(x/2)+C. The interval makes cosθ positive, which justifies dropping an absolute value.

Example 5 · Divide before partial fractions

Compute ∫x²/(x²+1) dx.

Polynomial division gives x²/(x²+1)=1−1/(x²+1). Integrating yields x−arctan x+C. Trying to fit the original improper rational fraction directly into partial fractions would hide this simpler decomposition.

Example 6 · An infinite endpoint

Compute ∫₁^∞x⁻²dx.

By definition this is the limit as R→∞ of [−1/x] from 1 to R, namely 1−1/R→1. The finite value is obtained only after the limit. More generally ∫₁^∞x⁻ᵖdx converges exactly when p>1.

Example 7 · An interior pole cannot be cancelled away

Does ∫₋₁¹1/x dx converge as an improper integral?

Split at zero. The integral from −1 to −ε is ln ε→−∞; the integral from ε to 1 is −ln ε→+∞. Since neither side converges finitely, the improper integral diverges. The symmetric principal value is zero, but it is a different definition.

Example 8 · Definite integration by symmetry

Compute ∫₋₂²(x³+cos x)dx.

The x³ part is odd and integrates to zero. Cosine is even, so the remaining contribution is twice ∫₀²cos x dx=2sin2. Symmetry removed work; it did not replace the domain check.

Example 9 · Moving endpoints

Differentiate G(t)=∫ₜ^(t²)e^(x²)dx.

The integrand is continuous, so the endpoint form of Leibniz applies. The upper contribution is e^(t⁴)·2t and the lower contribution is e^(t²)·1, with a minus sign. Thus G′(t)=2t e^(t⁴)−e^(t²). No elementary antiderivative of e^(x²) was needed.

Example 10 · The parameter trick, completed

Evaluate ∫₀¹(x−1)/ln x dx, interpreting both endpoint values by continuity.

Define F(a)=∫₀¹(xᵃ−1)/ln x dx for a≥0. The identity (xᵃ−1)/ln x=∫₀ᵃxᵗdt explains the removable value a at x=1. Differentiate with respect to a: F′(a)=∫₀¹xᵃdx=1/(a+1). Locally for a≥0 the derivative integrand is bounded by 1; near a=0 one can extend to a>−1/2 and bound by (1/√x), which is integrable. Thus the exchange is justified. Integrating the parameter derivative gives F(a)=ln(a+1)+C. Since F(0)=0, C=0. The requested value is F(1)=ln2.

Example 11 · Numerical integration when an antiderivative is unavailable

Use the midpoint rule with two equal subintervals to approximate ∫₀¹e^(−x²)dx.

The width is h=1/2 and the midpoints are 1/4 and 3/4. The estimate is [e^(−1/16)+e^(−9/16)]/2, approximately 0.7546. This is an approximation, not an exact antiderivative. Since |f″|=|(4x²−2)e^(−x²)|≤2 on [0,1], the composite midpoint error is at most 2/(24·2²)=1/48.

Problems 1–3 · Concept and mechanics

C02-P1

Compute indefinite integrals (a) ∫2x/(1+x²)dx and (b) ∫x ln x dx for x>0; verify by differentiation.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

(a) ln(1+x²)+C by substitution. (b) Parts with u=ln x and dv=x dx gives (x²/2)ln x−x²/4+C; differentiation recovers x ln x.

C02-P2

Compute (a) ∫₀¹dx/[(x+1)(x+2)] and (b) ∫₋₁¹(x³+x²)dx.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

(a) Partial fractions give ln(4/3). (b) The odd x³ part cancels; twice ∫₀¹x²dx gives 2/3.

C02-P3

Decide and evaluate when finite: (a) ∫₁^∞x⁻³dx, (b) ∫₀¹(1/√x)dx, (c) ∫₋₁¹x⁻²dx.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

(a) Limit of [−1/(2x²)]₁ᴿ is 1/2. (b) Limit of [2√x]ε¹ is 2. (c) Each side at zero diverges to +∞, so the improper integral diverges.

Problems 4–5 · Application and explanation

C02-P4

Compute ∫sin³x cos x dx and ∫dx/√(9−x²) on (−3,3). State the substitutions and domains.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The first uses u=sin x and gives sin⁴x/4+C. The second uses x=3sinθ with θ∈(−π/2,π/2) and gives arcsin(x/3)+C.

C02-P5

(a) Differentiate ∫₀^(t²)sin(x²)dx. (b) For F(a)=∫₀¹(xᵃ−1)/ln x dx, use the parameter derivative and F(0)=0 to evaluate F(2). State a justification for differentiating.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

(a) Chain rule plus FTC gives 2t sin(t⁴). (b) F′(a)=1/(a+1), so F(2)=ln3. An integrable local dominating bound for xᵃ around each a≥0 justifies differentiation, as in the worked example.

Exit check · close examples and solutions

Compute ∫01xex2 dx\int_0^1xe^{x^2}\,dx and verify an antiderivative.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

Use u=x² and du=2x dx. The answer is (e−1)/2. The derivative of e^(x²)/2 is xe^(x²).

If something broke

If a substitution loses a factor, differentiate the proposed answer. If bounds were mixed, write a two-row x/u table. Fresh repair: integrate 3x²/(1+x³) from 0 to 1; answer ln 2.

The big picture · reconstruct the map

Recognize substitution, integration by parts, and partial fractions, then check by differentiation. The map below connects the decisions, assumptions, and conclusions. Close the page and redraw its main branches from memory; then explain one connection with a worked example.

Text version of the map
  • Indefinite: Recover an antiderivative; Differentiate; include +C.
  • Proper definite: Signed accumulation on [a,b]; FTC; symmetry; substitution bounds.
  • Improper: Infinity or an unbounded integrand; Split singularities; take limits.
  • Choose a method: Substitution; parts; trig; fractions; Approximate if no elementary form.
  • Parameter viewpoint: Moving bounds: FTC + chain rule; Leibniz trick: justify the exchange.
