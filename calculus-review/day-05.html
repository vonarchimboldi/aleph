Opening check · before reading

For f(x)=x² on [1,3], what is the slope of the chord between the endpoints?

Average change and instantaneous change

Suppose a journey covers 100 kilometres in two hours. Its average speed is 50 kilometres per hour. Under suitable smoothness, some instantaneous speed must equal that average. For a graph, the mean value theorem says that some tangent has the same slope as the chord joining the endpoints.

If f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), with a<b, there is c in (a,b) satisfying:

f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−a.f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.

The closed interval appears with continuity because endpoint values determine the chord. The open interval appears with differentiability because the tangent point is interior. Endpoint derivatives are not required. Rolle’s theorem is the special case f(a)=f(b), where the chord is horizontal and some interior derivative is zero.

The useful move is often to rearrange the conclusion: f(b)−f(a)=f′(c)(b−a)f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a). If every derivative value is between m and M, the total change lies between m(b−a) and M(b−a). If |f′|≤L, then ∣f(b)−f(a)∣≤L∣b−a∣|f(b)-f(a)|\le L|b-a|. This controls output errors using input errors.

To derive strict increase from f′>0, choose any two distinct points a<b. MVT makes f(b)−f(a) a positive derivative times a positive length. This is why a derivative sign proves monotonicity on an interval. If f′=0 throughout an interval, the same argument gives equal endpoint values for every pair, so f is constant there.

A corner can invalidate the theorem. For |x| on [−1,1], the chord slope is zero, but the derivative is −1 on the left and 1 on the right, and does not exist at zero. There is no interior point with derivative zero.

Two worked examples · follow the decisions

Example 1 · 1 · Why the theorem matters

If f is differentiable on R and f′(x)=0 everywhere, can f(0) and f(1) differ?

MVT on [0,1] gives f(1)−f(0)=f′(c)·1=0. The derivative contains local slope information; the theorem translates it into a statement about two separated values. Differentiability on an interval is crucial.

Example 2 · 2 · Local extrema need not characterize zero derivatives

Compare f(x)=x², g(x)=x³, and h(x)=|x| at zero.

x² has a local minimum and derivative zero. x³ has derivative zero but no local extremum. |x| has a local minimum but no derivative. The theorem says a differentiable interior local extremum has derivative zero; it does not reverse that implication or remove differentiability.

Example 3 · 3 · Why an interior extremum has zero derivative

Let differentiable f have a local maximum at c. Why is f′(c)=0?

For small h>0, [f(c+h)−f(c)]/h≤0. For small h<0, the same nonpositive numerator divided by a negative number gives a quotient ≥0. If the two-sided derivative exists, it must be both ≤0 and ≥0, hence zero. At an interval endpoint there may be only one allowed side.

Example 4 · 4 · Find the actual global extrema

Find the extrema of x³−3x on [−2,2].

The function is continuous on a compact interval, so extrema exist. Interior critical points solve 3x²−3=0, giving ±1. Check those and endpoints: f(−2)=−2, f(−1)=2, f(1)=−2, f(2)=2. Thus maximum 2 at −1 and 2; minimum −2 at −2 and 1. Critical points alone would miss endpoint ties.

Example 5 · 5 · Rolle’s theorem

Find c for f(x)=x²−4 on [−2,2].

Continuity and differentiability hold, and endpoint values agree at zero. Rolle gives an interior zero derivative. Since f′=2x, that point is c=0. The equal endpoints, not zeros specifically, are the relevant condition.

Example 6 · 6 · Count zeros by counting possible turns

Prove x³−3x+4 has exactly one real zero.

The values at −3 and −2 are −14 and 2, so IVT gives a root in (−3,−2). The derivative is 3(x²−1), positive for x<−1, negative on (−1,1), positive for x>1. At the turning points the values are 6 and 2. Hence the function is positive for x≥−1 and strictly increasing on (−∞,−1), so the bracketed root is unique. Rolle also says two roots would force a derivative zero between them; it supplies upper-bound logic, while IVT supplies existence.

Example 7 · 7 · Locate the MVT point

For f(x)=x² on [1,3], find the tangent slope promised by MVT.

The chord slope is (9−1)/(3−1)=4. Solve f′(c)=2c=4, giving c=2 inside the interval. The theorem first guarantees such a c from its hypotheses; solving the equation then identifies it.

Example 8 · 8 · Prove MVT by subtracting the chord

How does Rolle’s theorem prove MVT for f on [a,b]?

Let m=[f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a) and h(x)=f(x)−f(a)−m(x−a). This subtracts the chord, making h(a)=h(b)=0. It preserves continuity and interior differentiability. Rolle gives h′(c)=0; since h′=f′−m, we obtain f′(c)=m. The auxiliary function was designed to match the theorem’s endpoint hypothesis.

Example 9 · 9 · Zero derivative and a disconnected-domain trap

Why can 1 for x>0 and −1 for x<0 have derivative zero without being one constant?

The domain is R without zero, which is not an interval. MVT proves constancy separately on each interval (−∞,0) and (0,∞), but cannot be applied between a negative and a positive input across the missing point.

Example 10 · 10 · Why antiderivatives differ only by a constant

If F′=f and G′=f on the same interval, show the relation between F and G.

For H=F−G, H′=0. MVT between any two points makes H constant, so F=G+C on that interval. This explains the +C in indefinite integrals. It does not, on its own, prove that every function possesses an antiderivative.

Example 11 · 11 · From derivative sign to monotonicity

Prove x+arctan x is strictly increasing on R.

Its derivative is 1+1/(1+x²)>0. Given any a<b, MVT gives f(b)−f(a)=f′(c)(b−a)>0. This quantifies why a local slope condition controls all pairs of inputs on the interval.

Example 12 · 12 · An interval sign chart

Find intervals of increase and decrease of f(x)=x³−3x.

The derivative is 3(x−1)(x+1). It is positive on (−∞,−1), negative on (−1,1), and positive on (1,∞). MVT converts those signs into increasing, decreasing, increasing intervals. At −1 there is a local maximum; at 1 a local minimum. Zero derivative at a boundary point does not defeat strict increase on an adjacent interval.

Problems 1–3 · Concept and mechanics

C05-P1

Find the MVT point for f(x)=x³ on [0,1].

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The chord slope is 1 and f′(c)=3c². The interior solution is c=1/√3; the negative algebraic solution is outside (0,1).

C05-P2

Apply Rolle’s theorem to f(x)=(x−1)(x−3) on [1,3], and find c.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The polynomial is continuous and differentiable and both endpoint values are zero. Its derivative is 2x−4, so the interior point is c=2.

C05-P3

For |x−1| on [0,2], which MVT hypothesis fails? Compare the chord slope with the existing derivative values.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The function is continuous but not differentiable at the interior point 1. The chord slope is zero, while existing derivative values are −1 or 1, never zero.

Problems 4–5 · Application and explanation

C05-P4

If 2≤f′(x)≤5 on [0,3] and f(0)=1, bound f(3). Assume differentiability on a containing open interval.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

MVT gives f(3)−1=3f′(c), between 6 and 15. Therefore 7≤f(3)≤16. The derivative bounds control total change, not f(3) directly.

C05-P5

For 0<a<b, prove (b−a)/b≤ln⁡b−ln⁡a≤(b−a)/a(b-a)/b\le\ln b-\ln a\le(b-a)/a.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

MVT for ln gives ln b−ln a=(b−a)/c for a<c<b. Since 1/b<1/c<1/a, the stated non-strict bounds follow (indeed the inequalities are strict).

Exit check · close examples and solutions

Use MVT to prove ∣arctan⁡b−arctan⁡a∣≤∣b−a∣|\arctan b-\arctan a|\le|b-a|.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The derivative is 1/(1+x²), whose absolute value is at most 1. Apply MVT between a and b, handle a=b separately, and take absolute values.

If something broke

If the theorem was applied across a corner, list the hypotheses before calculating. If derivative and function values were confused, draw a rectangle with height f′(c) and width b−a. Fresh repair: |√b−√a|≤(b−a)/2 for 1≤a<b.
