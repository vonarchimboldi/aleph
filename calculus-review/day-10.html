Opening check · before reading

State exactly what boundedness alone guarantees, and what monotonicity adds.

The same theorem can appear under different names

A recurrence is a sequence defined by a rule. A positive series is also a sequence problem: its partial sums are nondecreasing. Bisection builds two endpoint sequences. Recognizing which quantities form a sequence lets you use the same convergence ideas in several settings.

For a nonnegative series, every new term increases or preserves the partial sum. Therefore the series converges precisely when its partial sums are bounded above. “The terms are bounded” is not enough: harmonic terms are at most 1, yet their totals grow without bound. Be explicit about what is bounded.

In bisection, start with continuous f and f(a₁)<0<f(b₁). Test the midpoint. If it is a root, stop. Otherwise keep a half interval with opposite endpoint signs. The left endpoints form a nondecreasing sequence; the right endpoints form a nonincreasing sequence. Both are bounded. Their interval lengths halve at each step, so their limits agree at a common L. Continuity then makes f(L)=0. This connects Day 4 existence with a constructive approximation method.

A useful strengthening of Bolzano–Weierstrass is this: if a bounded real sequence has only one possible subsequential limit L, then the whole sequence converges to L. If it did not, some ε>0 would leave infinitely many terms at least ε away from L. Choose those terms as a subsequence. It is bounded, so Bolzano–Weierstrass gives a convergent further subsequence. Its limit still stays at least ε away from L, contradicting the assumed uniqueness. Boundedness is essential to this argument.

Two worked examples · follow the decisions

Example 1 · Partial sums turn a series into monotone convergence

Prove Σ1/[n(n+1)] converges by bounded partial sums.

Every term is positive, so Sₙ is nondecreasing. Decompose 1/[n(n+1)]=1/n−1/(n+1). Summing through N cancels all interior fractions and gives Sₙ=1−1/(N+1)<1. The increasing partial sums are bounded above, so monotone convergence proves a finite limit. The explicit expression identifies it as 1.

Example 2 · Bisection makes existence quantitative

Approximate the positive root of x²−2 on [1,2] for two bisections.

f is continuous and has signs − and + at 1 and 2. First midpoint 1.5 gives f=0.25>0, so retain [1,1.5]. Next midpoint 1.25 gives f=−0.4375<0, so retain [1.25,1.5]. The root lies inside that interval. Its midpoint estimate is 1.375 with error at most half the interval width, 0.125. After k bisections the retained width is 2^(−k); a midpoint estimate has error at most 2^(−k−1).

Problems 1–3 · Concept and mechanics

C10-P1

Let a₁=1 and aₙ₊₁=2−1/aₙ. Find the sequence and discuss whether a fixed-point equation is needed.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

a₁=1 gives a₂=1 and induction gives aₙ=1 for every n. The sequence is constant and converges to 1. The recurrence is well-defined since no term is zero. A direct invariant is simpler than solving a general limit equation.

C10-P2

Let aₙ=2+(-1)ⁿ/n. Is it monotone? Does it converge?

Worked solution — open after an attempt

It alternates above and below 2, so is not monotone. Since |aₙ−2|=1/n→0, it converges to 2. Monotone convergence is a sufficient theorem, not a necessary description of every convergent sequence.

C10-P3

Prove that the partial sums of Σ1/2ⁿ, n≥1, are bounded and nondecreasing; identify their limit.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

All terms are positive. The geometric finite sum is Sₙ=1−2^(−N)<1. Monotone convergence applies, and the expression gives limit 1.

Problems 4–5 · Application and explanation

C10-P4

Start bisection for x³+x−1 on [0,1]. Give the interval after two midpoint tests and a midpoint error bound.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

At 1/2 the value is −3/8, so retain [1/2,1]. At 3/4 it is 11/64>0, so retain [1/2,3/4]. Midpoint is 5/8 and guaranteed error at most 1/8. Continuity supplies root existence within each bracket.

C10-P5

A bounded real sequence has subsequences tending to 1 and −1. What can be concluded about convergence of the full sequence?

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The full sequence cannot converge, because all subsequences of a convergent sequence share its limit. The existence of those two subsequences alone does not exclude additional subsequential limits.

Exit check · close examples and solutions

Give (i) a bounded divergent sequence, (ii) an unbounded sequence with a convergent subsequence, and (iii) a bounded nondecreasing sequence. State which theorem applies to each.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

(i) (−1)ⁿ: Bolzano–Weierstrass guarantees a convergent subsequence, not full convergence. (ii) a₂ₖ=0, a₂ₖ₋₁=k: a convergent subsequence exists, but not through the boundedness hypothesis. (iii) 1−1/n: monotone convergence gives full convergence to 1; Bolzano–Weierstrass also applies, but is weaker here.
