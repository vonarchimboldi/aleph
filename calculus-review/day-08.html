Opening check · before reading

Why does the geometric expansion of 1/(1−x) need a restriction on x?

A family of series, one for each input

In a power series ∑n=0∞an(x−c)n\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n(x-c)^n, c is a fixed center and x is the input. Fix x and you have an ordinary numerical series. The set of x-values for which it converges is the question. Usually a ratio or root test gives a radius R: absolute convergence inside |x−c|<R and divergence outside. Check each endpoint separately; the radius alone does not decide them.

The geometric series is our starting model: 1/(1−x)=1+x+x2+⋯1/(1-x)=1+x+x^2+\cdots only for |x|<1. On that open interval, termwise differentiation and integration are valid for power series. They generate useful expansions, but boundary behavior must be checked again. Differentiation preserves the radius, not necessarily endpoint inclusion.

A Taylor polynomial matches a function’s derivatives at a center. At zero the quadratic polynomial is T2(x)=f(0)+f′(0)x+f′′(0)x2/2T_2(x)=f(0)+f'(0)x+f''(0)x^2/2. The coefficient 1/2 is needed because differentiating x² twice produces 2. In general the coefficient of xⁿ is f⁽ⁿ⁾(0)/n!.

A polynomial approximation is a finite statement with an error. Taylor’s theorem, when f has n+1 continuous derivatives on the segment between the center and x, writes f(x)=Tn(x)+f(n+1)(ξ)xn+1/(n+1)!f(x)=T_n(x)+f^{(n+1)}(\xi)x^{n+1}/(n+1)! for an intermediate ξ when centered at zero. Bounding that derivative gives a numerical error bound.

Having derivatives of every order does not by itself prove equality with an infinite Taylor series. Equality requires the remainder to tend to zero. For eˣ this can be shown on every bounded interval because factorial growth dominates the bounded derivative factor. In this refresher, justify approximation accuracy instead of silently replacing a function by an infinite sum.

Keep a few expansions connected to their origins

At zero, eˣ begins 1+x+x²/2!+x³/3!; sin x begins x−x³/3!; cos x begins 1−x²/2!. Their coefficients come from repeated derivatives at zero, and their Taylor remainders tend to zero for every fixed real x. The logarithm ln(1+x) begins x−x²/2+x³/3 and its power series is valid for |x|<1 (endpoint questions are separate). The geometric expansion of 1/(1−x) is valid for |x|<1. These domain statements are part of the formulas.

Two worked examples · follow the decisions

Example 1 · Endpoints ask two different questions

Find the convergence interval of ∑n=1∞xn/n\sum_{n=1}^\infty x^n/n.

For x≠0 the ratio of absolute consecutive terms is |x|n/(n+1), tending to |x|. Thus convergence is absolute for |x|<1 and fails for |x|>1. At x=1 we get the divergent harmonic series. At x=−1 we get a convergent alternating harmonic series. The interval is [−1,1); the radius is 1.

Example 2 · A polynomial with a certified error

Approximate e^(0.1) through the quadratic term.

All derivatives of eˣ at zero are 1, so T₂(0.1)=1+0.1+0.1²/2=1.105. Taylor’s remainder has magnitude at most e^(0.1)(0.1)³/6. Using the safe bound e^(0.1)<2 gives error less than 1/3000. The approximation and its guaranteed accuracy are separate outputs.

Problems 1–3 · Concept and mechanics

C08-P1

Find the radius and interval of convergence of ∑n=0∞(x−2)n/3n\sum_{n=0}^\infty(x-2)^n/3^n.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

It is geometric with r=(x−2)/3, so |x−2|<3: interval (−1,5), radius 3. At −1 the terms alternate ±1; at 5 they are 1. Neither endpoint has terms tending to zero.

C08-P2

Find the interval of convergence of ∑n=1∞xn/n2\sum_{n=1}^\infty x^n/n^2.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The ratio gives radius 1. At either endpoint the absolute series is Σ1/n², so both converge absolutely. Interval [−1,1].

C08-P3

Write the Taylor polynomial of degree 3 for sin x at zero.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The derivatives at zero are 0,1,0,−1, giving x−x³/6. This is a finite polynomial, so no convergence interval is needed merely to write it.

Problems 4–5 · Application and explanation

C08-P4

Find lim⁡x→0(ex−1−x)/x2\lim_{x\to0}(e^x-1-x)/x^2 using Taylor’s theorem.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

Write eˣ=1+x+x²/2+R₂(x), with R₂(x)/x²→0 because its magnitude is bounded by a constant times |x|³ near zero. The limit is 1/2.

C08-P5

Integrate the geometric series to obtain an expansion for −ln(1−x), and state a domain where the derivation is valid.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

For |x|<1 integrate termwise from 0 to x: −ln⁡(1−x)=∑n=0∞xn+1/(n+1)-\ln(1-x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^{n+1}/(n+1). The constant is zero because both sides vanish at zero. Endpoint equality needs a separate argument.

Exit check · close examples and solutions

Find the interval of convergence of ∑n=1∞(x−1)n/(n2n)\sum_{n=1}^\infty (x-1)^n/(n2^n).

Worked solution — open after an attempt

Let r=(x−1)/2. The ratio gives |r|<1, or −1<x<3. At x=−1 it is alternating harmonic and converges; at x=3 it is harmonic and diverges. Interval [−1,3).
