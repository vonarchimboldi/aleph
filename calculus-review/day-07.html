Opening check · before reading

The terms 1/n tend to zero. Does that alone prove that their sum is finite?

A series is a sequence of totals

Writing a₁+a₂+… indefinitely is not a finite addition. Form the partial sums SN=∑n=1NanS_N=\sum_{n=1}^N a_n. The series converges when this sequence of totals approaches a finite real number. The terms aₙ and the totals Sₙ answer different questions.

For a convergent series, aₙ must tend to zero because aₙ=Sₙ−Sₙ₋₁. The converse is false: harmonic terms 1/n tend to zero, but the harmonic series diverges. Grouping terms from n=2ᵏ+1 to 2ᵏ⁺¹ gives 2ᵏ terms, each at least 1/2ᵏ⁺¹. Every block adds at least 1/2, preventing finite total accumulation.

A geometric series ∑n=0∞rn\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^n converges to 1/(1−r) exactly when |r|<1. The p-series ∑n=1∞1/np\sum_{n=1}^\infty1/n^p converges exactly when p>1. These provide comparison standards. For nonnegative terms, if 0≤aₙ≤bₙ eventually and Σbₙ converges, so does Σaₙ. To prove divergence, bound below by a known divergent nonnegative series.

The ratio test is useful with factorials and exponentials: compute the limiting ratio of absolute consecutive terms. A limit below 1 proves absolute convergence; above 1 proves divergence. A limit equal to 1 is inconclusive. The root test makes the same distinction using the limit superior of |aₙ|^(1/n). Neither test replaces checking endpoint cases in power series.

For alternating terms, the alternating-series test requires the positive magnitudes to decrease eventually and tend to zero. It then proves convergence, with error at most the next omitted magnitude. Absolute convergence means Σ|aₙ| converges; it implies convergence. Conditional convergence means the signed series converges while the absolute series diverges.

Two more recognition examples

For Σ(n/(2n+1))ⁿ, the nth root of the positive nth term is n/(2n+1), tending to 1/2. The root test proves convergence. Exponents involving n often suggest this test.

For Σ1/(n ln n), starting at n=2, a ratio test gives 1 and stalls. The positive function 1/(x ln x) is decreasing for x≥2, so the integral test applies. Its integral from 2 to R is ln(ln R)−ln(ln2), which grows without bound. The series diverges. State positivity, continuity, and eventual decrease before using this integral comparison.

Two worked examples · follow the decisions

Example 1 · A comparison that respects direction

Decide ∑n=1∞1/(n2+n)\sum_{n=1}^\infty1/(n^2+n).

The terms are positive and at most 1/n², so comparison proves convergence. We can do more: 1/[n(n+1)]=1/n−1/(n+1). The Nth partial sum telescopes to 1−1/(N+1), which tends to 1. The series converges and its sum is 1.

Example 2 · Convergent is not the same as absolutely convergent

Classify ∑n=1∞(−1)n+1/n\sum_{n=1}^\infty(-1)^{n+1}/n.

The magnitudes 1/n decrease to zero, so the alternating-series test gives convergence. The absolute series is the harmonic series and diverges. Therefore convergence is conditional. A ratio limit of 1 would not settle either series.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

This is geometric with |r|=2/3<1. Its sum is 1/(1−2/3)=3.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The terms tend to 1, not zero, so the necessary condition fails and the series diverges. No more elaborate test is needed.

This is a p-series with p=3/2>1, so it converges.

Problems 4–5 · Application and explanation

C07-P4

Decide ∑n=1∞n/2n\sum_{n=1}^\infty n/2^n using the ratio test.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The ratio is (n+1)/(2n), tending to 1/2<1. Thus the positive series converges. Its sum is not required for this test.

C07-P5

Decide ∑n=1∞(−1)n/n\sum_{n=1}^\infty(-1)^n/\sqrt n. Is convergence absolute?

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The magnitudes decrease to zero, so the alternating test proves convergence. The absolute series is a p-series with p=1/2≤1, which diverges. Convergence is conditional.

Exit check · close examples and solutions

Decide ∑n=1∞n2/3n\sum_{n=1}^\infty n^2/3^n, and state what your test proves.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The ratio is ((n+1)/n)²/3, tending to 1/3<1. The series converges absolutely (its terms are already positive).
