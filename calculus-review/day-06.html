Opening check · before reading

Sketch x² and −x². Which graph lies below a chord joining two points?

Reading companion: Spivak’s geometric route

Read Michael Spivak, Calculus, fourth edition, Chapter 11 appendix, “Convexity and Concavity,” pp. 219–229. This is an original companion, not a reproduction of the chapter or its exercises. The chapter’s route compares chords and tangents, relates them to changing slopes, and then uses curvature to interpret graphs. Its convention calls the strict chord property “convex”; it later calls the non-strict property “weakly convex.” Our notes use the common modern convention: “convex” allows equality and “strictly convex” excludes it at interior chord points. Keep this dictionary beside the reading.

First draw the comparison we want to make

Pick two graph points (a,f(a)) and (b,f(b)), with a<b, and join them by a straight segment. At an intermediate input x, the segment has height ℓ(x), while the curve has height f(x). The whole question is which height is larger. A convex graph lies at or below the segment. A concave graph lies at or above it. An upward bowl such as x² is convex even where it is decreasing.

Write x=(1−t)a+tb, with 0≤t≤1. The chord’s height is the matching weighted average (1−t)f(a)+tf(b). Therefore convexity means:

f((1−t)a+tb)≤(1−t)f(a)+tf(b).f((1-t)a+tb)\le(1-t)f(a)+tf(b).

For strict convexity, replace ≤ by < when a≠b and 0<t<1. Equality is unavoidable at the endpoints. Concavity reverses the inequality; multiplying a concave function by −1 makes it convex.

Secant slopes are another representation of that picture

For a<x<b, let m₁ be the slope from a to x and m₂ the slope from x to b. The curve lying below the full chord is equivalent to m₁≤m₂. To see the algebra, multiply by the positive lengths x−a and b−x, expand, and rearrange:

(b−a)f(x)≤(b−x)f(a)+(x−a)f(b).(b-a)f(x)\le(b-x)f(a)+(x-a)f(b).

Divide by b−a to recover exactly the chord height comparison. The full-chord slope is a weighted average of m₁ and m₂, so it lies between them. A geometric statement about heights has become an ordered statement about slopes.

Let the secants approach a tangent

If f is differentiable, shrinking a secant toward x turns its slope into f′(x). The secant inequalities then give, for a<b:

f′(a)≤f(b)−f(a)b−a≤f′(b).f'(a)\le\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\le f'(b).

In particular f′ is nondecreasing. Rearranging the left inequality shows f(b)≥f(a)+f′(a)(b−a): the graph lies above the tangent at a. On the other side of a, the sign of b−a reverses the needed slope comparison, and the same supporting-line inequality results. A convex curve lies below its chords but above its tangents. These are compatible because a tangent is a limiting local line, not a chord joining two separated graph points.

Why increasing slopes imply the chord picture

Now suppose f is differentiable and f′ is nondecreasing. Apply MVT separately to [a,x] and [x,b]. It identifies their secant slopes as f′(u) and f′(v), with a<u<x<v<b. Since f′ is nondecreasing, f′(u)≤f′(v). Thus m₁≤m₂, which we already translated into the chord inequality. This supplies the converse without needing a second derivative.

There is also a converse from supporting tangents. If every tangent lies below the graph, write the supporting inequality once from a to b and once from b to a. After division by b−a, the chord slope is sandwiched between f′(a) and f′(b). Hence f′ is nondecreasing, and the preceding argument proves convexity.

The second derivative is a shortcut through the same chain

If f is twice differentiable and f″≥0 on an interval, MVT applied to f′ makes f′ nondecreasing. That gives convexity by the earlier argument. If f″>0, it gives strictly increasing slopes and strict convexity. A strict inequality f″>0 everywhere is sufficient, not necessary: x⁴ is strictly convex even though f″(0)=0.

Curvature alone does not tell you whether values increase or decrease. The first derivative answers that question. For −ln x on x>0, f′=−1/x<0 and f″=1/x²>0: the graph decreases while bending upward. At an inflection, the graph changes concavity in the usual smooth examples. A zero of f″ merely supplies a candidate; it is not a guarantee.

What the geometry buys us

If f is convex and differentiable and f′(c)=0, the supporting-line inequality becomes f(x)≥f(c), proving a global minimum on the interval. Strict convexity ensures at most one minimizer. An affine function is both convex and concave and can have a whole interval of minima, so dropping “strict” changes the uniqueness claim.

A useful consequence of chord geometry is the midpoint inequality, the two-point case of Jensen’s inequality. For concave ln it yields ln((a+b)/2)≥(ln a+ln b)/2; exponentiating gives the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality. The direction of the chord comparison determines the direction of the resulting numerical bound.

Two worked examples · follow the decisions

Example 1 · Separate rising from bending

Classify ln x on (0,∞).

The first derivative 1/x is positive, so the function increases. The second derivative −1/x² is negative, so the function is strictly concave. Increasing output and decreasing slopes are compatible: each further step still adds output, but adds less than the previous step.

Example 2 · An inflection requires a sign change

Find the concavity intervals of f(x)=x³−3x.

Differentiate twice: f′=3x²−3 and f″=6x. Thus f″<0 for x<0 and f″>0 for x>0. The function is concave on (−∞,0) and convex on (0,∞). Curvature changes at the graph point (0,0), so it is an inflection point. The stationary points ±1 answer a different question.

Problems 1–3 · Concept and mechanics

C06-P1

For f(x)=x², verify both the chord inequality and supporting tangent inequality by computing their differences.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

The chord gap is (1−t)a²+tb²−[(1−t)a+tb]²=t(1−t)(a−b)²≥0, strictly positive when a≠b and 0<t<1. The tangent gap at a is x²−[a²+2a(x−a)]=(x−a)²≥0.

C06-P2

Use MVT on [a,x] and [x,b] to prove that a differentiable function with nondecreasing derivative is convex.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

MVT gives the two secant slopes as f′(u) and f′(v) with u<v. Monotonicity makes the first no larger. Multiplying by the positive lengths and rearranging yields (b−a)f(x)≤(b−x)f(a)+(x−a)f(b), the chord inequality.

C06-P3

Classify −ln x on (0,∞) as increasing/decreasing and convex/concave. Write its supporting tangent at x=1.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

f′=−1/x<0 gives decrease; f″=1/x²>0 gives strict convexity. At 1 the value is zero and slope −1, so the tangent is 1−x and −ln x≥1−x. Equivalently ln x≤x−1.

Problems 4–5 · Application and explanation

C06-P4

Explain why x⁴ is strictly convex but has no inflection at zero. Compare it with x³.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

For x⁴, f′=4x³ is strictly increasing, so the MVT secant argument gives strict convexity. Its second derivative 12x² does not change sign at zero. For x³, f″=6x changes sign from negative to positive there, so zero is an inflection point.

C06-P5

Use concavity of ln to derive AM≥GM for positive a,b. State the equality case and why it follows.

Worked solution — open after an attempt

Apply the concave midpoint inequality, then exponentiate to get (a+b)/2≥√(ab). Strict concavity makes equality possible only when a=b. The domain a,b>0 is necessary for the logarithms.

Exit check · close examples and solutions

Find intervals of convexity/concavity and any inflection point of x³−6x².

Worked solution — open after an attempt

f″=6x−12. It is concave for x<2 and convex for x>2. Curvature changes at x=2, where f(2)=−16, giving inflection point (2,−16).

If something broke

If curvature was inferred from f′, compute f″ explicitly. If f″=0 was taken as sufficient for inflection, contrast x³ with x⁴. Fresh repair: −ln x is decreasing and convex on x>0.

Text version of the map
  • Chord geometry: Convex below chords; Concave reverses the inequality.
  • Secant translation: Left slope ≤ right slope; Full chord lies between them.
  • Tangent view: Nondecreasing derivative; Graph above supporting tangents.
