After recording A0: open the teaching · 12 minutes

1. A dependent choice is not one fixed choice

Read ∀x∈R ∃y∈R (y=x+3) as a game. First someone chooses any real x. Then you may choose a real y after seeing x. The rule y=x+3 always works: x=2 needs y=5; x=−7 needs y=−4.

In ∃y∈R ∀x∈R (y=x+3), you must commit to one y before x is chosen. If you choose y=c, someone can choose x=c, demanding c=c+3. That is impossible. Changing the order changes which choices may depend on earlier choices.

2. Negation challenges the claim inside the same world

The domain tells us what objects we are discussing. Negating “every integer…” does not move us to nonintegers. It asks whether some integer defeats the assertion.

ClaimNegation
Every allowed x has property PSome allowed x does not have P
Some allowed x has property PEvery allowed x fails P
∀x∈D ∃y∈E P(x,y)∃x∈D ∀y∈E ¬P(x,y)

Example: negate ∀n∈Z ∃m∈Z (m≥2n). First switch ∀ to ∃ and ∃ to ∀; keep Z twice; finally replace ≥ with <. Result: ∃n∈Z ∀m∈Z (m<2n). Read it aloud: there is an integer n for which every integer m is less than 2n.

3. A grammatical negation and a truth judgment are separate jobs

“There exists a real a such that every real x satisfies x>a” is false. Whatever a is proposed, x=a is allowed and does not satisfy x>a. Its negation is ∀a∈R ∃x∈R (x≤a), true using x=a. Notice: the witness rule is more informative than a single numerical example.

Use four checks: domains unchanged; quantifiers switched; whole predicate negated; result read back in words.

After recording B0: open the teaching · 10 minutes

1. Name the two propositions before rearranging

Let P mean “12 divides n” and Q mean “4 divides n.” An implication says: whenever P is true, Q must be true.

NameFormExample
OriginalP⇒Q12|n⇒4|n
ConverseQ⇒P4|n⇒12|n
Contrapositive¬Q⇒¬P4∤n⇒12∤n

The original is true: n=12k gives n=4(3k). The converse is false at n=4. The contrapositive is equivalent to the original: if 4 does not divide n, then 12 cannot divide n, because divisibility by 12 would force divisibility by 4.

“P does not imply Q” denies a universal implication. It is not its converse. To refute P⇒Q, find a case with P true and Q false. If P is false, that case cannot refute the implication.

2. For inequalities, preserve the exact predicates

If P is “x>3” and Q is “x²>9,” then ¬P is “x≤3” and ¬Q is “x²≤9.” Converse: x²>9⇒x>3, false at x=−4. Contrapositive: x²≤9⇒x≤3, true. Replacing Q by ¬Q without changing the rest creates a different statement.

3. A proof covers all permitted cases

Every integer is even or odd. If n=2k, n²=4k² is even. If n=2k+1, n²=2(2k²+2k)+1 is odd. These exhaustive cases prove that n and n² have the same parity. Seeing several numerical squares would not prove this.

4. Multiplication by an expression needs its sign

For (x−1)/(x+2)>0, first exclude x=−2. The numerator vanishes at x=1. On x<−2 both factors are negative, giving a positive quotient. On −2<x<1 their signs differ. On x>1 both are positive. Thus x<−2 or x>1; neither boundary is included. Multiplying by x+2 without knowing its sign could reverse the inequality unnoticed.
